Mexico City is the highest stop on the Formula 1 calendar, and the thin air is why the weekend feels different from a sea-level race. Teams run more wing than the long straight seems to ask for, they open the bodywork to cool the car, and the braking zones get harder. What the sources do not agree on is the exact height.

[Pirelli's preview](https://press.pirelli.com/mexico-new-asphalt-and-the-softest-compound-trio/) for this year's race, published October 28, calls the venue unique because it sits more than 2,000 meters above sea level. Other guides for the same circuit use tighter numbers, and they are not the same number.

| Source | Figure it gives |
| --- | --- |
| McLaren circuit guide, 2026 | 2,285 meters (7,497 feet) |
| Formula 1 technical article, 2019 | 2,250 meters |
| McLaren race preview, October 28, 2026 | 2,200 meters |
| Pirelli, October 28, 2026 | more than 2,000 meters |

McLaren's [circuit guide](https://www.mclaren.com/racing/formula-1/2026/mexico-city-grand-prix/) also names Interlagos as the next highest track in that comparison, at 800 meters (2,625 feet). The spread in the table is about the survey figure. It is not a disagreement about the effect. Each of these sources describes less air than a sea-level circuit.

## What less air does to the car

A [Formula 1 technical article from 2019](https://www.formula1.com/en/latest/article/tech-tuesday-why-its-the-air-that-poses-the-biggest-challenge-in-mexico.6qKZ7LcCKBT7xPTihVjIoq) put the air in Mexico City at about 25 percent less dense than at a sea-level circuit such as Bahrain. On that account, the same wings make about a quarter less downforce and about a quarter less drag, so a large wing costs less straight-line speed than it would at sea level. That piece was written for the 2019 cars. It explains the air. It is not a 2026 wind-tunnel result, and this site is not treating the 25 percent figure as a new measurement.

McLaren's [2026 circuit guide](https://www.mclaren.com/racing/formula-1/2026/mexico-city-grand-prix/) describes the same kind of weekend without that percentage. It says a turbocharged Formula 1 car does not lose power the way a naturally aspirated engine would, though the turbo has to work harder. It says the thinner air reduces aerodynamic grip and cooling, so teams run more wing and a more open car than they would at sea level.

[Pirelli's 2026 note](https://press.pirelli.com/mexico-new-asphalt-and-the-softest-compound-trio/) makes the same aero point in the language of tires. Less aerodynamic load means more tire slip, and graining is something to watch. The rear axle is the one it flags as most stressed. Pirelli also says the altitude makes the heaviest braking zones harder to manage, and that the long straight into Turn 1 can raise the chance of a lock-up early in the weekend, when grip is still low.

## The straight, and what this does not predict

McLaren's [race preview](https://www.mclaren.com/racing/formula-1/2026/mexico-city-grand-prix/preview/) says the lap is 4.304 kilometers with 17 corners, and that the main straight is about 1.2 kilometers. That is the passing zone the thin air creates: less drag on the straight, less grip in the braking zone at the end of it. Our [Mexico City preview](/articles/2026-10-27-mexico-city-gp-preview-us-fans/) and the [US start times](/articles/2026-10-27-mexico-city-gp-race-times-us/) cover the weekend logistics. The [Red Bull power-unit story](/articles/2026-10-28-red-bull-ford-engine-supply-mekies/) is where we logged how the engine makers talk about this round.

None of this is a forecast of who will be fast on Sunday. Pirelli says the track's low severity could favor a one-stop race, and it also says the new asphalt on much of the lap still has to be read in practice. Those are the supplier's expectations, not a result.
