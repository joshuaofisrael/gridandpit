The Mexico City Grand Prix is always run in thin air, but this year the paddock is treating it as an unknown. It is the first time the new 2026 power units have raced at extreme altitude, and drivers spent Thursday's [FIA press conference](https://www.fia.com/news/f1-2026-mexico-city-grand-prix-thursday-press-conference-transcript) guessing how it will shake out.

## How high is it?

The circuit sits roughly 2,200 meters above sea level, more than 7,000 feet, as [Sky Sports](https://www.skysports.com/f1/news/13603997/lewis-hamilton-reveals-hes-lost-out-in-recent-f1-races-with-set-up-of-his-ferrari-car-compared-to-charles-leclerc) and [F1 Oversteer](https://www.f1oversteer.com/races/mexico-city-gp-2026-what-kimi-antonelli-needs-to-win-f1-title-how-to-watch-and-weather-forecast/) both note. For comparison, Denver is about 1,600 meters. At that height each breath of air holds noticeably less oxygen, and that affects three things on a race car: the engine, the wings and the cooling.

## What turbo lag means

A turbocharger uses exhaust gas to spin a compressor that forces extra air into the engine. In thin air it has to spin harder to deliver the same amount, and it takes longer to get up to speed when the driver gets back on the throttle. That delay is turbo lag.

The 2026 rules dropped the heat energy recovery motor that used to sit on the turbo shaft and keep it spinning, so teams have less help hiding the delay. Mercedes driver George Russell said on Thursday: "I think turbo lag is going to be a big factor for a lot of teams this weekend." He added that "the likes of Ferrari have a smaller turbo than most, so they probably have quite an advantage in this regard." A smaller turbo generally spins up faster, which is why it may respond better out of slow corners.

Russell was careful not to overstate it. "We're not sure as yet how challenging it will be, but I can definitely foresee it being not straightforward," he said.

## Less air means less downforce

Wings work by pushing on air, so thinner air means less grip from the same wing. Cadillac's Sergio Perez put a number on it. "We're losing around 100 points or something like that with the altitude, so we end up going quite slowly," he said, describing the loss in the units engineers use to measure downforce. That is why teams run big wings here and the cars still slide around.

Perez said that also makes the lap less physical than you might expect: "There isn't a lot of high speed... We have very long straights."

## Why it can mix up the order

Haas driver Oliver Bearman said he does not know whether altitude will help or hurt his team's engine, and that "the only way is to find out tomorrow." He also pointed out a racing effect from last year, when he finished fourth: in the thin air "you struggle to follow the other cars," which helped him hold position. Alpine's Pierre Gasly said he hopes altitude can play a factor and give the midfield a chance at surprises.

For US fans, that makes Friday practice worth watching for clues.
